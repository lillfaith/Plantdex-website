# Do the genus cards' claims generalise? — the four in-genus species

Each species legitimately belongs to its card's genus, so no exclusion follows from taxonomy.
The question is whether **the card's own printed content** stays true for someone holding that
species. Classification is one of four, as specified.

**Evidence standard used here.** Human evidence and animal-specific toxicity are separated
throughout, because for two of these four the hard toxicology is veterinary and reading it as
human risk — or dismissing it because it is not — would both be wrong.

---

## Sambucus ebulus (dwarf elder) → **SHOULD BE EXCLUDED FROM CARD SCOPE**

**Card #31 Elderberry** — parts **Berry, Flower, Leaf, Shoot**; preparations **Tea, Infusion,
Spice, Cold soak**; traits *Antiviral, Immune boost, Respiratory support, Fever aid*.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Toxicity | Ribosome-inactivating proteins with lectin activity (**ebulin**) in all parts; cyanogenic glycoside; saponins |
| Human evidence | **Yes.** A peer-reviewed review states its *"usefulness as food is restricted due to its toxicity"*; high-dose fruit consumption induces **vomitory toxicity, especially in children**. It appears in paediatric acute-plant-exposure casework. |
| Mitigation | **Cooking.** Short-term heating removes the lectin risk. |

**Two things make this an exclusion rather than a caution.**

1. **The card's `Cold soak` preparation is explicitly uncooked, and heat is the single documented
   mitigation.** The card does not merely omit a warning; it names a method that defeats the one
   thing that would make the berry safe.
2. **The card's four traits are the *S. nigra* / *S. canadensis* elderberry profile.** *S. ebulus*
   belongs to a different medicinal tradition, used differently and in different preparations. A
   player handed this card for a dwarf elder is not reading a card with a gap in it — they are
   reading a card about another plant.

**The existing `SITE_CAUTIONS` #31 does not cover it.** That caution addresses Leaf and Shoot and
then says *"the flowers and cooked ripe berries are the parts traditionally used"* — which remains
wrong for *S. ebulus*, whose **ripe berries** are the restricted part.

---

## Sambucus racemosa (red elderberry) → **SAFE WITH CARD CAUTION / WORDING CHANGE**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Toxicity | Sambunigrin (cyanogenic) in leaves and unripe fruit; a **nauseant resin in the seeds** of all *Sambucus*, destroyed by cooking |
| Human evidence | **Yes.** Raw berries, leaves, twigs and seeds cause **nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea**; large amounts cause lethargy, dizziness, drowsiness. Described as *"the most toxic of the three Sambucus species, but its toxicity is confined to a tummy ache."* |
| Mitigation | Cook thoroughly **and strain out the seeds** |

This is a real elderberry with a genuine food use once cooked and strained, so excluding it would
deny a legitimate find. But the card's **`Cold soak`** is again the exact wrong instruction, and
the seed caution applies to **every** *Sambucus* — including the species the card was written for.

**Required caution:** raw berries and seeds cause gastrointestinal upset; cook and strain.

---

## Pinus ponderosa → **SAFE WITH CARD CAUTION / WORDING CHANGE**

**Card #41 Pine** — parts **Needle**, Resin, Cone, Shoot; preparations **Tea**, Steam, Syrup,
Infusion.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Animal evidence | **Strong.** **Isocupressic acid** is the compound isolated and shown to cause abortion in pregnant beef cattle; third-trimester abortion follows within 3-4 days. |

> **CORRECTION, after this audit first shipped.** It said ponderosa was *"the only *Pinus* known
> to cause abortion in cattle"*. That came from a USDA framing about naturally-occurring field
> cases and is **not true as a general statement**: isocupressic acid occurs across a range of
> North American gymnosperms, and **lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*) has caused abortion in
> controlled feeding trials** — cows dosed at 62-78 mg ICA/kg bodyweight aborted after 8 and 10
> days — as has common juniper (190-245 mg/kg, 3-4 days). Ponderosa has the strongest and
> best-known evidence; it is not the only implicated species. The caution text was corrected
> with it.
| Human evidence | **Ethnobotanical and precautionary, not clinical.** Indigenous peoples used the inner bark and seeds **but knew that pregnant women should not chew the buds or needles, because it would cause a miscarriage.** Conifer teas are generally described as safe in moderation, **with ponderosa named as the exception.** |

**The exposure route matches the card exactly: needle, as tea.** That is what moves this above a
footnote — the card names the one part and the one preparation the caution is about.

**Being explicit, since the distinction matters:** the controlled toxicology is **bovine**. There
is no human clinical dataset. What exists on the human side is a consistent traditional
prohibition pointing at the same organ system as the animal data, plus a compound with a known
mechanism. That is enough for a pregnancy caution and not enough for an exclusion.

## Acer rubrum (red maple) → **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE**

**Card #17 Maple** — parts Sap, **Leaf**, Bark; preparations Sap, Syrup, **Tea**, Infusion;
compounds Quebecol, **Gallic acid**, Catechins, Sucrose.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Animal evidence | **Strong, equine-specific.** Wilted red maple leaves cause Heinz-body haemolytic anaemia and methaemoglobinaemia in horses; the implicated agent is **gallic acid**. Threshold **>1,500 mg/kg bodyweight** of wilted leaves. **60-65% mortality** among affected horses (retrospective study, 32 cases). |
| Human evidence | **None found.** Searches returned equine literature and low-quality commercial/forum pages; no human case reports, no authoritative human-toxicity source. |

**The card names the implicated compound as a feature.** *Gallic acid* is printed as a signature
compound of the Maple card, and it is the agent in the equine syndrome. That is a genuinely
uncomfortable coincidence and it is not, by itself, evidence of human risk.

**Why this is `INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE` and not `SAFE`:**

- The equine dose is enormous — a horse eating on the order of a kilogram of wilted leaves — and
  the mechanism turns on erythrocyte susceptibility to oxidative damage, which is species-specific.
  Reading it across to a human drinking leaf tea is not supported.
- But **nor is the card's Leaf claim supported**. I could not find an authoritative source for
  human leaf-tea use of *any Acer*; the Sap and Bark claims are well attested and the **Leaf** one
  is the weak link — and that is a question about the card, not about *A. rubrum*.

**What would settle it:** a toxicological review of *Acer* leaf preparations for human consumption,
or an authoritative ethnobotanical source for maple-leaf tea. Neither was reachable.

---

## Does the Sambucus card's content generalise across its genus? **No.**

Asked directly, the honest answer is that this is **not merely a wording problem** for one card.

- Its four traits are the *S. nigra*/*S. canadensis* profile and do not describe *S. ebulus*.
- Its `Cold soak` preparation is wrong for **at least two** of the three species in scope.
- It already carries a `SITE_CAUTIONS` entry because its printed **Leaf** and **Shoot** are unsafe
  for the species it *was* written for.

Three independent content defects on one card, two of which bite hardest on the species it was
not written for. **One species must leave the scope, and the caution the other two need is about
raw berries and seeds, not about leaves.**

Pine and Maple are different: their content largely does generalise, and the gaps are a pregnancy
caution (Pine) and a weakly-attested Leaf claim (Maple, and for the whole genus rather than one
species).
